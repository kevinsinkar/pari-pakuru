KÁ RUSUT•ERIT?

Lesson 14 — Did You See That?

Blue Book pp. 90–95

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it. Some letters follow English habits (for example d for the soft Pawnee r).
  • Dictionary entry — the learner spelling from the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation. UPPERCASE marks the accented syllable, the one Parks marks with an accent in his phonetic form.
  • IPA — the exact scholarly transcription recorded by Douglas Parks from fluent speakers. When the other spellings disagree, the IPA is the authority.

Grammar & Notes

Adjectives

ru ti ra•párihrit ru tuks•ta•párihrit ti kasis ti katus ta rara•rásitu' ta ta rá•ritu' tá taris kaká taris it is smooth it was smooth it's hard it's flat it's cold = indicator "this" it's hot it's heavy it's light (not heavy) Antonyms In English many adjectives come in pairs: big/small, light/ heavy. These opposites are called antonym s. In Pawnee, almost any adjective can be made into its antonym by using the negative indicator kaki or kaka. More on Indicators We have already pointed out that the model indicator ti is used for third person subjects, ta for first and second person. In the same way, the negative model indicator kaki is used for third person subjects, kaka for first and second persons. tat•at I went (have gone) tas•at you went tí•'at he went kakat•at I didn't go kakas•at you didn't go kakI 'at he didn't go In addition, there are adjective and verb roots which take the indicator ta, even though the subject may be third person. ta•rutsa' he (has) lifted it ta rara•rasitu it's cold ta rá•ritu' it's hot tá taris it's heavy Notice that these verb phrases imply an effect on the subject, like heat or weight.

Useful Phrases

1. Hais•tiwata! (SK) Look! Su'ks•tewata! (SB) Look! 2. Hais•atku! (SK) Listen! Su'ks•atku! (SB) Listen! 3. Ka ris•utsi•urikat! (SK) Don't touch! Ka res .- iI urtikat! (SB) Don't touch! SUBJECT AND OBJECT: Transitive Verbs The noun that refers to the doer is the subject. The noun that refers to the done-to thing is the object. Verbs that describe actions like seeing and killing may appear with both subject and object: these are called transitive verbs. The subject always appears before the object, if both are named by a noun; the English order subject-verb-object is acceptable, but old Pawnee speakers preferred both nouns together . Pita ti•kuwutit kuruks. The man has killed a bear. Pita kuruks tí•kuwutit. The man has killed a bear. TRANSITIVE VERB SET: Tut•erit. He has seen it (he saw it). Question: Take rut•erit? Tatut•érit. Reflexive Marker

The reflexive indicator witi means that the subject is also the object. Tatut•erit. Witi tatut•erit. Animal Voices Rikutski ti•wáktahu'. Asaki tirah•kí•wat. Taraha kata•kaksa'. Arusa ti•kis•kata•wakta'. Kuruks ti•wáktahu'. Ti•kuruks•páktahu'. I saw it. I saw myself. The bird is singing. The dog barked. The buffalo bellowed. The horse neighed. The bear is growling. He is growling (bear-talking).
