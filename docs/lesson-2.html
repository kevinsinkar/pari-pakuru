KIRIKJ RA•TARAWIS?

Lesson 2 — What Color Is it?

Blue Book pp. 33–39

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it. Some letters follow English habits (for example d for the soft Pawnee r).
  • Dictionary entry — the learner spelling from the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation. UPPERCASE syllables carry high pitch, which changes meaning in Pawnee.
  • IPA — the exact scholarly transcription recorded by Douglas Parks from fluent speakers. When the other spellings disagree, the IPA is the authority.

Dialogues

Kirike ra•tarawis rakis? What color is the stick?
Rakis ti pahaat. The stick is red.
Ka ra•pahaat rakis? Is the stick red?
Ahu', rakis ti pahaat. Yes, the stick is red.
Ka rd .tareus rakis? Is the stick blue?
Kaki tareus, ti pahaat. It is not blue, it is red.
NOUN PHRASE: Noun•Adjective
A word which describes or qualifies a noun is an adjective.
Words, for example, like red, tall, heavy, are adjectives. In Paw-
nee, adjectives can come directly after the noun in a noun
phrase, as in the following examples.
kiriks•pahat
a red bead
kuruks•katit
a black bear.
Noun phrases like those above can be used as proper names.
Or, as in the examples below, they can be used as statements.
Ti kiriks•pahat. It's a red bead.
(The bead is red.)
Ti kuru'ks•katit. It's a black bear.
(The bear is black.)
Sometimes the noun will stand separately outside the adjec-
tive phrase, as in:
Kiriks ti pahaat. The bead is red.
Kuruks ti katiit. The bear is black.

Dialogue lines are shown in the Blue Book's practical spelling, exactly as printed.

Grammar & Notes

A Sound Change

Many Pawnee roots begin with r. This beginning r will change to a t if the last letter of the root before it in a phrase ends with any of the following hard consonants: p, t, k, ts, or s. Note the following example: rakis + rahkata = rakis•tahkata a yellow stick

Pronunciation

To speak Pawnee well, you must learn to draw out or lengthen certain vowels. It is not possible to make a simple rule that will tell you when to do this. So listen carefully to speakers of Pawnee and to the tapes which accompany these lessons. Listen for the vowels which are drawn out and those which are not. In this way you will learn to pronounce the words in a genuine Pawnee fashion. Here are some examples of simple words with both long and normal (short) vowels in the first syllable of the word: Short Vowels rakis kiriks nawa riwit (rd-kis) (ki-riks) (rd-wa) (ri-wit) wood bead now skunk Long Vowels Pari' (pd-ri ') Pawnee hitu ' (hi-tu ') feather raku' (ra-ku') box kitsu ' (ki-tsu ') liquid In addition, the Skidi pronunciation of a word may keep a syllable short where South Band pronunciation lengthens it. Suks•paku' (SB) (suks pa-ku') You (1) say it! (SK)(suks pd-ku ') You (1) say it! As a serious student of the Pawnee language, you should listen for and imitate these long syllables, and try to get the right quality of the vowel. To help in remembering a long syllable, use a pencil to underline the vowel of the long syllable right in your book. raku ' or raku ' box

Note On Research

When you write down words you hear from Pawnee speakers, be sure to underline the long syllables.

Interpretation

Perhaps the most interesting and enjoyable part of studying Pawnee is learning how to interpret Pawnee words, or how to understand the meaning of a word by figuring out its smaller parts or roots. Notice how the two words for snake can be interpreted by comparing them to other words in the lesson. rutki (SB) snake rut + ki = serpent + small thing = snake rakis stick, piece of wood rak + kis = wood + small thing = stick Most Pawnee words are derived or made up from small roots. One of these small roots used to make up words is + kits + or + ki +, which means liquid. kitsu ' liquid kits + u' hetu ' breast het•kitsu ' milk rasitu' ice kira•rasitu' water ki + ra + rasitu ' liquid from ice In addition, most objects in nature, which Engish may name simply river or tree, will be named in Pawnee by a descriptive phrase: ra .ha•pi tree or "it's wood standing" ti r I ha•pi There is a tree. + u + in a line + hat + goes by ti kits•u"hat stream, river or "it's liquid going by"
