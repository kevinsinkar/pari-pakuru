TARIKTAU'

Lesson 5 — The Body

Blue Book pp. 49–55

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it. Some letters follow English habits (for example d for the soft Pawnee r).
  • Dictionary entry — the learner spelling from the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation. UPPERCASE syllables carry high pitch, which changes meaning in Pawnee.
  • IPA — the exact scholarly transcription recorded by Douglas Parks from fluent speakers. When the other spellings disagree, the IPA is the authority.

Grammar & Notes

Notes

Position of Objects When talking about any object in Pawnee, you must consider its position or attitude: your head "sits, " (+ ku) almost every other part of your body "hangs" (+ ta). Spelling Rule When i follows u, it drops out. So ti + ku + iks = tiku 'ks, and the remainder of the root joins the introducer, in this case.

More On The Verb Phrase

A verb phrase is any group of written words which includes an introducer. To avoid confusion, we will talk of introducers being composed of markers; every other meaningful part of a verb phrase will be a root or will be composed of roots.

Passive Introducers

To say "it hurts" Pawnee uses a form of the introducer which means "it does it to me." ti + I'm telling you that it .. . + ku . . . to me (person marker, first person object) tiku + perks + taari' it does it to me + head + hurts Tiku•paks•taari'. My head hurts.

Physical Possession

To express physical possession of body parts, the marker ri + is used in the introducer, following the modal marker (indicator) ti, and followed by the person marker. mode + phys. poss. + person + noun + verb ti + ri + t + kirik + to Ti rit•kirik•ta. Here is my eye.

Completed And Incomplete Action

The simplest form of a verb is the perfect form. In this form the verb indicates action or events already completed. Tiku'ks•taa. My hand has been hurt. (my hand got hurt) Perfect Tiku'ks•taari'. My hand is hurting. (my hand hurts) Imperfect The imperfect form indicates action or events still happening, incomplete action. This is the form used in this chapter, though the perfect form could easily be substituted in the dialogues.

Commands

In Pawnee, commands to the hearer use the perfect form of the verb, and build an introducer out of the second person (hearer) marker s +, the command marker +uks, and number markers si + (two hearers) and +rak (three or more hearers). Irregular Roots

Normally, the root form of any particular verb, say eat, will stay the same, no matter who is eating or when they ate. However, some common verbs in Pawnee will have different forms for different subjects. Notice the roots for go are +at for one person, +wa (+pa) for two persons, and +wu (+pu) for three or more persons as subjects. This is somewhat like English I go and I went, rather than I goed. *SPELLING RULE Remember, w goes to p after a hard consonant, and k drops out before p.
