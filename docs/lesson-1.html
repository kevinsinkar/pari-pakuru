KIRIKÉ RU'?

Lesson 1 — What Is It?

Pari Pakuru' (1979), pp. 1–5

Basic words Dialogues More words Useful phrases Notes Exercises

The order is the book's: a few basic words, then a dialogue that uses them. Learn the words, read the dialogue aloud, both parts, then cover one side and say it from memory.

Which spelling do I learn to write? The one under In the dictionary: the learner spelling, with â î û for long vowels, ' for the catch in the throat, c for “ts” and č for “ch”. The Blue Book column is how the 1979 textbook spells the same word, and Say it is a rough guide for English speakers, not a spelling. Dialogues and phrases are printed only in the textbook's spelling, so they stay in it here. Where a teacher or a speaker writes a word another way, follow them.

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it, with a raised dot between the parts of a word.
  • Entry — the dictionary entry the word could be tied to, in the learner spelling of the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels. Where no entry could be tied to a word, the cell says so; the word may still be in the dictionary under its stem.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation of that dictionary entry. UPPERCASE marks the accented syllable, the one Parks marks with an accent in his phonetic form.
  • Phonetic form — the transcription Douglas Parks prints for the entry. When the other spellings disagree about a sound, this is the authority.

Dialogues

Kiriké ru'? What is it?
Tí hitu'. This is a feather.
Ka rú hitu'? Is this a feather?
Ahu', tí hitu'. (SB) Yes, this is a feather.
Haú, tí hitu'. (SK)
Ka rú karitki? Is this a rock?
Kakí, kakí karitki. No, it is not a rock.
Kiriké ru'? What is it?
Tí hitu'. This is a feather.

Grammar & Notes

Nouns

There are very few short words in Pawnee. Most words are more descriptive and are longer constructions of smaller parts. The shorter words are usually names of common objects, such as the things named in this chapter. Words that name things are called nouns, and nouns in Pawnee often end in the suffix -u'. (A suffix is a small part of a word attached to the end of it.) Some nouns in Pawnee are words which have been copied or borrowed from English; when this happens, the word is given a distinct Pawnee pronunciation, and often it is hard to hear the English equivalent. Look at the names for nickel, dime, and quarter above. What English words do they suggest?

Pronunciation

At first, you may find some sounds of Pawnee hard to pronounce. One of the sounds is the letter r. It is easier to pronounce between vowels. There it sounds like a fast d as in the English word ready, but is softer. Drill: (iri) kiriks (ari) karitki (ira) tirakis When the r begins a word, it is helpful to stick in a very short a just before the r, so that you put the r between vowels. Drill: rakis (arakis) raku' (araku')

Useful Phrases

1. Nawa! Hello, okay, now. The old pronunciation of this word is rawa, but the pronunciation and spelling with n is so common, we have included this word as the only one to use n. Common expressions tend to receive unique pronunciations. Notice that the Skidi word for yes (haú) ends with a strong nasal quality (sound coming through the nose). 2. Istu. Again. 3. Suks•páku'. Say it. 4. Aka! Oh my! Gee! 5. Ka? What?

Indicators

Indicators are Pawnee words which indicate statements, questions, denials, and so on. Ká ru'? Ka rú kisu'? Ti'. Tí kisu'. Kakí'. Kakí kisu'. Is it? Is it a bone? It is. This is. This is a bone. It is not. No. It is not a bone.
