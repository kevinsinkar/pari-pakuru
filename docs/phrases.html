Phrases to use today

Short things to say at home, at the table and to each other. All 113 are the “Useful Phrases” of the textbook Pari Pakuru' (1979), gathered here by situation, with the two conversations the book asks every learner to know by heart.

Which spelling do I learn to write? These phrases are printed only in the 1979 textbook, so they stand here in the textbook's spelling, letter for letter; the raised dot marks the parts of a word. For single words, learn the learner spelling the dictionary pages put first (â î û for long vowels, ' for the catch in the throat). Where a teacher or a speaker writes a word another way, follow them.

Skiri or South Band? The textbook writes for both dialects of Pawnee. Its spelling and its accent marks follow South Band speakers, and it marks a form SK or SB where the two differ. A line marked SB spelling here is written with e or hr: the book says a Skiri speaker says i for that e, and h for hr. This page does not respell such a line.

What the textbook says about the two dialects
  • 3 KIRIKÉ RUT•U'? (SB) / KÍRIKU RUT•U'? (SK) / What Does It Look Like? (contents page: a lesson title printed in both dialects)
  • “Some vowels, however, will vary in length and quality depending on who is speaking and what dialect is being used.” (p. xviii: vowels differ with the dialect)
  • e (e) pet (SB) akse cow / pit (SK) (ák-si) cow (p. xviii: the letter e in the alphabet table: South Band as in 'pet', Skidi as in 'pit')
  • (e̲) fiancé (SB) parésu' hunter / receipt (SK) (pa̲-rí-su') hunter (p. xviii: long e: South Band as in 'fiancé', Skidi as in 'receipt')
  • silent (SB) het•kítsu' milk (p. xviii: h at the beginning of a word is silent for South Band speakers)
  • silent after h (SK) tsahriks person (p. xix: r is silent after h for Skidi speakers)
  • “The combination hr is a breathy version of r which is pronounced simply as h by Skidi speakers, so that the r is silent.” (p. xx: hr is said h by Skidi speakers)
  • “Silent h: South Band speakers do not pronounce h at the beginning of words; the h will be silent as in English honor.” (p. xx: South Band speakers do not say h at the beginning of a word)
  • “There are four main vowels in Pawnee, though the South Band e is pronounced by Skidi speakers just as if it were an i.” (p. xxi: the vowel written e is South Band; Skidi speakers say i)
  • “You will find that South Band speakers have more long vowels than do Skidi speakers, so you should decide which dialect you will concentrate on, and mark your textbook for the particular sounds of that dialect.” (p. xxii: South Band speakers have more long vowels; the learner chooses a dialect)
  • “This is especially true for South Band speakers. Skidi speakers are more likely to glide two vowels together. […] Notice the Skidi pronunciation sounds like English cow.” (p. xxiii: two vowels together: South Band speakers break between them, Skidi speakers glide)
  • “IMPORTANT. The accent marked in this book is that of some South Band speakers. There will be other speakers who use a slightly different accent.” (p. xxiv: the accent marks of the book are South Band)

The Pawnee Nation's own first lesson says the same of the vowels: “Vowels (South Band or SB: 8 & Skiri: 6)”, and that e and ee are South Band sounds. Where to find that lesson.

Two conversations to know by heart

Here are two conversations which should be memorized and practiced by anyone who wants to be able to identify himself as a Pawnee speaker or encourage elders to speak Pawnee. Alternate phrases are in parentheses.
Pari Pakuru', lesson 20

Two people, A and B. A line with no letter goes on from the line above. Take both parts aloud.

Conversation 1

A Nawa, taku ká ra•ka•ku? Hello, is anyone home?
B Nawa, síks•uka•a'. Hello, come in.
Suks•pitit, tireku•skúskakusu. SB spelling Sit down. Here's a chair.
A Hiri we túr•rahe. SB spelling Thank you.
B Ka rás•kika•ista rekíts•katit? SB spelling Do you want (will you drink) coffee?

Conversation 2

A Nawa, Tiwatsiriks. Hello, Uncle.
B Nawa, tsikstit ka rás•ku? (Tsikstit ka rás•pari?) Hi, are you well? (Are you doing well?)
A Ahu', tsikstit tát•ku, (Tsikstit tat•pari',) Yes, I'm fine, (I'm doing fine,)
a tsikstit ka rasta•pí•hat? and are you all (family) doing well?
B Ahu', tsikstit tahra•pi•hat. SB spelling Yes, we're fine.
Kirike rusutsíks•ari'? SB spelling What have you been doing?
A Tikú•riusis•ari ratku•wá•waktit Pari' ... I've been learning to speak Pawnee ...
(Parí pakuru ... ratara•kuki.) (the Pawnee language ... in our way.)
B Ka ras•í•waktits? Rarit ka rás•paku, Pari'? Is that true? Do you speak it well, Pawnee?
A Rarít kakat•paku', ketsi ríku•riusis•ari. SB spelling I don't speak well, but I'm learning.
B Nawa, kirike hawá rasut•ari'? SB spelling Okay, what else are you doing?

About these phrases

Every phrase and its English is printed in the textbook, in the lesson named beside it; this page copies them and makes none. Putting them under situations is this site's arrangement, not the book's. Where the book prints one phrase in two lessons with two English renderings, both are shown.

(1), (2), (3+). The book explains: “When speaking Pawnee you use a different form for you when addressing one person, two people, or three or more people. In this book we will use (1), (2), or (3+) after the you to show how many people are referred to.” (lesson 2). (SK) and (SB). The book marks a form of the Skiri band (it writes “Skidi”) with (SK) and a form of the South Bands with (SB). One South Band form is left out here because two of its letters stand under a flaw in the scan; lesson 14 shows it with the gap marked.

No speaker of Pawnee has reviewed this page, and it cannot let you hear a phrase. If a speaker says one of these another way, theirs is the one to learn. Where to learn with people, and where to hear the language.
