KIRIKÉ RAS•TA?

Lesson 4 — What Do You Have?

Pari Pakuru' (1979), pp. 15–21

Basic words Dialogues More words Useful phrases Notes Exercises

The order is the book's: a few basic words, then a dialogue that uses them. Learn the words, read the dialogue aloud, both parts, then cover one side and say it from memory.

Which spelling do I learn to write? The one under In the dictionary: the learner spelling, with â î û for long vowels, ' for the catch in the throat, c for “ts” and č for “ch”. The Blue Book column is how the 1979 textbook spells the same word, and Say it is a rough guide for English speakers, not a spelling. Dialogues and phrases are printed only in the textbook's spelling, so they stay in it here. Where a teacher or a speaker writes a word another way, follow them.

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it, with a raised dot between the parts of a word.
  • Entry — the dictionary entry the word could be tied to, in the learner spelling of the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels. Where no entry could be tied to a word, the cell says so; the word may still be in the dictionary under its stem.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation of that dictionary entry. UPPERCASE marks the accented syllable, the one Parks marks with an accent in his phonetic form.
  • Phonetic form — the transcription Douglas Parks prints for the entry. When the other spellings disagree about a sound, this is the authority.

Grammar & Notes

Pronunciation

Remember that in the Skidi manner, an r is silent when it follows an h. táh•ra = (tá-ha) (SK) (tá-hra) (SB) The combination r•r is actually pronounced as a single r.

Pronunciation

Remember that within a phrase, the last consonant of a root is slurred into the beginning of the next root. pitkus•íksapits = (pit-ku-sík-sa-pits)

Verb Phrase

Words which refer to actions or events are called verbs. In English, have is a verb; in Pawnee, táh•ra is a verb phrase, a group of roots meaning I have it. The first part, tab ., is an introducer, the second, •ra, the verb root meaning have. Introducers In Pawnee, an introducer carries information such as 1) MODE (indicators or markers) how does this information relate to what the speakers know? 2) PERSON: (markers) who is involved? 3) NUMBER: (markers) how many are involved? introducer•verb = mode + person + verb root táh•ra ta + h + ra I'm telling you + I + have I have it. ta +s +ra I'm telling you + you + have You have it. ra + s + ra we know + you + have (that) you have it. introducer•verb = mode + person + number + verb root táhrar•ra ta + h + rar + ra I tell you + I + them + have I have some (them). 'INTRODUCER = MODE + PERSON + NUMBER Mode can be indicated either by a marker or an indicator which is joined to the introducer. The indicator may change its last letter according to who is the subject. Táh•ra. I have it. Kakáh•ra. I don't have it. VERB SET: Táh•ra I have it. (May be used to play a classroom game like "Who's got the button?") Question: Také ra•ra? Who has it? Responses: Táh•ra. I have it. Tás•ta. You have it. Tí•ra. He or she has it. tás•ta rás•ta Question: Také rarar•ra? Responses: Tahrár•ra. Tastár•ra. Tírar•ra. Si táhrar•ra. Si tástar•ra. Si tírar•ra. Tahráktar•ra Tastáktar•ra Tíhrar•ra Who has them? I have them. You have them. He or she has them. We (2) have them. You (2) have them. They (2) have them. We (3+) have them. You (3+) have them. They (3+) have them.

Plurals

In English, a noun which refers to more than one thing is called a plural noun, and it ends in -s, the plural suffix. (Examples: dogs, cats, trees.) In Pawnee, there is really no plural ending, and any noun can refer to one thing, two things, or three or more things. pita a man, some men rakis a stick, some sticks hitu' a feather, some feathers Plurality is usually indicated by markers in the introducer of the verb phrase. Táh•ra. I have it. Táhrar•ra. I have some. + rar + plural (more than one) Tahráktar•ra. We have them. + rak + more than one of us Usually, the plural subject is marked by + rak +, the plural object by + rar +. There are other plural markers such as + ir + for they and + atsir + for two of us, but these plurals are used in only a few places. Tihrar•ra. They (3+) have them. ti + ir + rar•ra Tatsíh•wari'. We (you and I) are going about. ta + atsir + wari'
