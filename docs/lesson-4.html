KIRIKJ RAS•TA?

Lesson 4 — What Do You Have?

Blue Book pp. 43–49

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it. Some letters follow English habits (for example d for the soft Pawnee r).
  • Dictionary entry — the learner spelling from the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation. UPPERCASE syllables carry high pitch, which changes meaning in Pawnee.
  • IPA — the exact scholarly transcription recorded by Douglas Parks from fluent speakers. When the other spellings disagree, the IPA is the authority.

Grammar & Notes

Pronunciation

Remember that in the Skidi manner, an r is silent when it follows an h. tah•ra = (td-ha) (SK) (td-hra) (SB) The combination rr is actually pronounced as a single r.

Pronunciation

Remember that within a phrase, the last consonant of a root is slurred into the beginning of the next root. pitkus•iksapits = (pit-ku-sik-sa pits)

Useful Phrases

1. Kitske'? How much? How many? 2. Turrahe It's good. 3. Kak'tirrahe. It's bad. (no good) 4. Siks•a'. Come here. (you, 1) 5. Siksut•tsa'. Stand up. (you, 1) 6. Ruru ' . (SB) Go (on). (you, 1) Hiru'. (SK)

Verb Phrase

Words which refer to actions or events are called verbs. In English, have is a verb; in Pawnee, t ih-ra is a verb phrase, a group of roots meaning I have it. The first part, tab ., is an introducer, the second, •ra, the verb root meaning have. Introducers In Pawnee, an introducer carries information such as 1) MODE (indicators or markers) how does this information relate to what the speakers know? 2) PERSON: (markers) who is involved? 3) NUMBER: (markers) how many are involved? introducer•verb = mode + person + verb root tah•ra ta + h + ra I'm telling you + I + have I have it. ta +s +ra I'm telling you + you + have You have it. ra + s + ra we know + you + have (that) you have it. introducerverb = mode + person + number + verb root ta'hrarra ta + h + rar + ra I tell you + I + them + have I have some (them). 'INTRODUCER = MODE + PERSON + NUMBER Mode can be indicated either by a marker or an indicator which is joined to the introducer. The indicator may change its last letter according to who is the subject. Ta'h•ra. I have it. Kakah•ra. I don't have it. VERB SET: Tah .ra I have it. (May be used to play a classroom game like "Who's got the button?") Question: Take ra•ra? Who has it? Responses: Tah•ra. I have it. Tas•ta. You have it. TI ra. He or she has it. tas-ta ras•ta Question: Take rararra? Responses: Tahrarra. Tastarra. Tirarra. Si tahrarra. Si tastarra. Si tirarra. Tahraktarra Tastaktarra Tihrarra Who has them? I have them. Plurals

In English, a noun which refers to more than one thing is called a plural noun, and it ends in -s, the plural suffix. (Examples: dogs, cats, trees.) In Pawnee, there is really no plural ending, and any noun can refer to one thing, two things, or three or more things. pita a man, some men rakis a stick, some sticks hitu ' a feather, some feathers Plurality is usually indicated by markers in the introducer of the verb phrase. Tah•ra. I have it. Thhrarra. I have some. + rar + plural (more than one) Tahra'ktarra. We have them. + rak + more than one of us Usually, the plural subject is marked by + rak +, the plural object by + rar +. There are other plural markers such as + it + for they and + atsir + for two of us, but these plurals are used in only a few places. Tihrarra. They (3+) have them. ti + it + rarra Tatsih•wari'. We (you and I) are going about. to + atsir + wari'
