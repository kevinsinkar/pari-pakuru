KIRÚ RA•'AT

Lesson 9 — Where Has It Gone?

Pari Pakuru' (1979), pp. 42–46

Basic words Dialogues More words Useful phrases Notes Exercises

The order is the book's: a few basic words, then a dialogue that uses them. Learn the words, read the dialogue aloud, both parts, then cover one side and say it from memory.

Which spelling do I learn to write? The one under In the dictionary: the learner spelling, with â î û for long vowels, ' for the catch in the throat, c for “ts” and č for “ch”. The Blue Book column is how the 1979 textbook spells the same word, and Say it is a rough guide for English speakers, not a spelling. Dialogues and phrases are printed only in the textbook's spelling, so they stay in it here. Where a teacher or a speaker writes a word another way, follow them.

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it, with a raised dot between the parts of a word.
  • Entry — the dictionary entry the word could be tied to, in the learner spelling of the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels. Where no entry could be tied to a word, the cell says so; the word may still be in the dictionary under its stem.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation of that dictionary entry. UPPERCASE marks the accented syllable, the one Parks marks with an accent in his phonetic form.
  • Phonetic form — the transcription Douglas Parks prints for the entry. When the other spellings disagree about a sound, this is the authority.

Dialogues

Ka rá•awari rikutski? Is the bird (still) flying around?
Kakí', rikutski tí•'isat. No, the bird has disappeared.
Kirú ra•'at rikutski? Where has the bird gone?
Ti•káisat. It flew into the woods.

The lines are in the Blue Book's spelling, as printed; they were read from a scan of the book line by line. A line without English is printed without one (a second dialect's form, or another way to say the line above).

NOUNS IN THE SENTENCE: WORD ORDER Remember, there are two main kinds of nouns you may find in a sentence, subjects (refer to who is doing it) and objects (refer to who or what is experiencing it, but not doing anything). Kiwaku wé túh•kuksas. The fox ran away.

Object Verb Phrase

If there is only one noun in the sentence, it can come either before or after the verb, according to what is the most important new information in the sentence. Rikutski tí pahaat. The bird is red. Tí pahaat rikutski. The bird is red. Normally, what is important or in focus is first in the sentence; what is understood or presumed is last. When two nouns appear in the same sentence, the subject comes directly before the verb phrase, the object comes last, and time words may follow the object. Pita ti•kuwutit rahurahki pitsikat. subject verb phr. object time word The man killed a deer , in the winter.
