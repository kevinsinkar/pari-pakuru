Lesson 20 — I Want To Speak Pawnee.

Blue Book pp. 111–130

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it. Some letters follow English habits (for example d for the soft Pawnee r).
  • Dictionary entry — the learner spelling from the Parks Dictionary, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation. UPPERCASE syllables carry high pitch, which changes meaning in Pawnee.
  • IPA — the exact scholarly transcription recorded by Douglas Parks from fluent speakers. When the other spellings disagree, the IPA is the authority.

Grammar & Notes

Interpretation

I want it, I want to .. . for (someone) to ... (secondary phrase marker) tatpd .waktit I have spoken + wa + of various things ratku•w&waktit Pari for me to speak Pawnee ratara•kuki (in) our (band's, tribe's) way raku•wa .waktit ratara•kuki for one to speak in our way tat•itska ra( )ku +

Primary And Secondary Verb Phrases

Each of the verb phrases we have studied so far has been a primary verb phrase, which means it can be spoken by itself as a complete sentence. When two verb phrases are used together as in tatitska ratkti u•ta I want to go, there is a primary and also a secondary phrase. In English, we often use the word to to mark these verbs. In Pawnee, the introducer of a secondary phrase includes the markers ra + and +ku, with the person marker coming in between. In this chapter, we deal only with the future form of secondary verbs, the form that would naturally go with the primary verb: I want to (do something in the future). There are other forms and meanings of secondary verbs. raku•wa .waktit to speak (a language) (secondary, perfect) we raku•sakur•isat•ta when the sun goes down (see Lesson 17) raku•ka .pihat dwelling (see Reading)

Cultural Note

It is traditional Pawnee courtesy to serve coffee or other refreshments without asking the guest whether or not he or she would like some. If the guest shows up at dinner time, he is immediately shown to a place at the table. No opportunity is given for polite refusal, as is the European custom, and visitors are expected to visit before doing business.

Epilogue

Our Way (Our People) LIVING PAWNEE: Speaking, Singing, Writing. The Pawnee language lives when people talk to each other in their own way. Speech and conversation are the main ways but not the only ways people talk to each other. Singing and writing are important ways in which people talk with or communicate to other people. Listening to songs and reading any written material can help you learn Pawnee. The special Pawnee language material in this chapter can be used for classroom or private enjoyment of this written, sung and recited material.

Where We Used To Live

This our Pawnee tribe is made up of four bands; we are these: Tsawii, Kitkahahki, Pitahawirata and Tskiri. And it (the tribe) used to live, it is said, in mud lodges near the Platte ("Flat") River. Twice a year they went hunting. It is said that they killed buffalo and deer, (game). In addition, they planted various things, corn, beans and squash. They would roast corn, and used to make jerky and they dried the beans and squash. Perhaps there were about thirty people dwelling in one mud lodge. Also they would put their horses inside in the winter. It is said that it (the tribe) had (many) customs which were very wonderful. The old ones were apparently sincere (about these things). It must have been good land there where we lived. Those other enemy tribes about (us), attacked us all the time. The leaders, perhaps, decided to come down here to the south. a B u
