KIRÚ RA•KU?

Lesson 8 — Where Is It?

Pari Pakuru' (1979), pp. 37–42

Basic words Dialogues More words Useful phrases Notes Exercises

The order is the book's: a few basic words, then a dialogue that uses them. Learn the words, read the dialogue aloud, both parts, then cover one side and say it from memory.

Two spellings of one word. Under In the Skiri dictionary a word stands as the dictionary spells it: a long vowel written double (aa), c for both “ts” and “ch”, and ʔ for the catch in the throat. The switch at the top of the page, Spelling, changes it to the learner spelling: â î û for long vowels, ' for the catch, c for “ts” and č for “ch”. That is the alphabet of the Pawnee Nation's own lesson handouts (where to find them). The Blue Book column is how the 1979 textbook spells the same word, and Say it is a rough guide for English speakers, not a spelling. Dialogues and phrases are printed only in the textbook's spelling, so they stay in it whatever you choose. Where a teacher or a speaker writes a word another way, follow them.

Skiri or South Band? The textbook writes for both dialects of Pawnee. Its spelling and its accent marks follow South Band speakers, and it marks a form SK or SB where the two differ. A line marked SB spelling here is written with e or hr: the book says a Skiri speaker says i for that e, and h for hr. This page does not respell such a line. The dictionary on this site is Skiri: where a word is tied to an entry, the entry is the Skiri word.

What the textbook says about the two dialects
  • 3 KIRIKÉ RUT•U'? (SB) / KÍRIKU RUT•U'? (SK) / What Does It Look Like? (contents page: a lesson title printed in both dialects)
  • “Some vowels, however, will vary in length and quality depending on who is speaking and what dialect is being used.” (p. xviii: vowels differ with the dialect)
  • e (e) pet (SB) akse cow / pit (SK) (ák-si) cow (p. xviii: the letter e in the alphabet table: South Band as in 'pet', Skidi as in 'pit')
  • (e̲) fiancé (SB) parésu' hunter / receipt (SK) (pa̲-rí-su') hunter (p. xviii: long e: South Band as in 'fiancé', Skidi as in 'receipt')
  • silent (SB) het•kítsu' milk (p. xviii: h at the beginning of a word is silent for South Band speakers)
  • silent after h (SK) tsahriks person (p. xix: r is silent after h for Skidi speakers)
  • “The combination hr is a breathy version of r which is pronounced simply as h by Skidi speakers, so that the r is silent.” (p. xx: hr is said h by Skidi speakers)
  • “Silent h: South Band speakers do not pronounce h at the beginning of words; the h will be silent as in English honor.” (p. xx: South Band speakers do not say h at the beginning of a word)
  • “There are four main vowels in Pawnee, though the South Band e is pronounced by Skidi speakers just as if it were an i.” (p. xxi: the vowel written e is South Band; Skidi speakers say i)
  • “You will find that South Band speakers have more long vowels than do Skidi speakers, so you should decide which dialect you will concentrate on, and mark your textbook for the particular sounds of that dialect.” (p. xxii: South Band speakers have more long vowels; the learner chooses a dialect)
  • “This is especially true for South Band speakers. Skidi speakers are more likely to glide two vowels together. […] Notice the Skidi pronunciation sounds like English cow.” (p. xxiii: two vowels together: South Band speakers break between them, Skidi speakers glide)
  • “IMPORTANT. The accent marked in this book is that of some South Band speakers. There will be other speakers who use a slightly different accent.” (p. xxiv: the accent marks of the book are South Band)

The Pawnee Nation's own first lesson says the same of the vowels: “Vowels (South Band or SB: 8 & Skiri: 6)”, and that e and ee are South Band sounds. Where to find that lesson.

Why do the spellings differ?

The same Pawnee word appears in several writing systems, and they are all telling you the same thing:

  • Blue Book — the practical spelling printed in Pari Pakuru' (1979), exactly as the Pawnee Cultural Retention Committee wrote it, with a raised dot between the parts of a word.
  • Entry — the dictionary entry the word could be tied to, in the dictionary's own spelling, with a long vowel written double (aa). The switch at the top of the page, Spelling, changes it to the learner spelling, with a circumflex (â) marking long vowels. Where no entry could be tied to a word, the cell says so; the word may still be in the dictionary under its stem.
  • Say it — the simplified pronunciation of that dictionary entry. UPPERCASE marks the accented syllable, the one Parks marks with an accent in his phonetic form.
  • Phonetic form — the transcription Douglas Parks prints for the entry. When the other spellings disagree about a sound, this is the authority.

Grammar & Notes

Past Tense

There is one marker in Pawnee which refers to time. This is the marker +uks, which follows the person marker in the introducer and means in a time past. We have seen that +uks also marks the command form, but in that usage, the introducer has no marker of mode. Suks•átku. Tasuks•átku. Listen! You listened. The u in +uks is a strong vowel which causes the a in modal marker +ra and i in modal marker ti + to drop out. question mode person past verb kiru +ra +ø +uks +ku kiru +ra +uks +ku (a drops out) Kíru ruks•ku? Where was it? Pawnee does not use a letter or root to refer to the third person. It's the fact that no person marker is present which means third person. The symbol is used to denote the absence of a marker in the interpretation. tat•kíkat tat• = ta + t tas•kíkat tas• = ta + s ti•kíkat ti• = ti + ø I am crying first person you are crying second person he, she, it is crying third person Notice also that the modal marker has a different vowel for third person. mode person past root root ti +1 +uks +ka +ku' ti +uks +ka +ku' Tuks•kaku'. He was in the house.

Nouns In A Verb Phrase

In Pawnee, a noun can often be included inside the verb phrase, as we saw in Lesson 3. That was a case of subject nouns being included. Nouns of location can also be included. First they are reduced to a root form and then inserted before the verb. 3g hatur•ráru' road ti•káarit he is standing in it ti +hatur +ka +arit r becomes h before k +ka + "in" ti +hatuh +ka +arit ti•hatúh•kaarit he's standing in the road
